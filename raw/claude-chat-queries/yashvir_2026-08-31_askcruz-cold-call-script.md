Assistant: You're right to flag it. Two things wrong with it, not one.

"Feel free to tell me if now's a bad time" isn't labeling, it's an open door. Voss's actual technique is naming the tension without handing over an exit — "I know this is out of the blue" acknowledges it; "feel free to tell me if now's bad" is a scripted permission slip for the prospect to hang up before you've said anything of value. I conflated the two. That's a real flaw, not a stylistic nitpick.

"I'll be quick either way" is worse. It's a hedge — it apologizes for taking their time before they've objected to anything. That directly contradicts the Belfort certainty-in-delivery point I flagged as worth keeping in Section 2. An opener shouldn't sound like it's bracing for rejection.

Net effect: the line I wrote sounds honest, but it's honest in a way that reads as low-status and gives the prospect a scripted off-ramp in the first breath. That's a real design flaw, and it's the kind of thing that would only surface once you're actually running it — which is worth naming as its own risk: none of this script has field data behind it yet, this whole call is a hypothesis.

Tighter version, same transparency, no exit door:

"Hi [Name], it's Yash — I'll be straight with you, this is a cold call. I've got one specific question about [pain area]. You got 20 seconds?"

Still labels it as unannounced (keeps the Voss disarm), but ends on a specific, closed ask instead of an open invitation to bail. "20 seconds" is also a harder, more concrete unit than "quick" — concrete beats vague when you're asking for something.

Want this swapped into the script, or do you want to stress-test it further first?

Assistant: The philosophies pull in opposite directions on the exact flaw we just fixed.

Jeremy Miner, NEPQ style

"Hey [Name], this is Yash with [Company]... hey, listen, I know I'm calling out of the blue — if you've gotta run in the next 20 seconds, just tell me and I'll let you go, okay?"

NEPQ's whole design is to sound unlike a salesperson — deliberately unpolished, lower energy, imperfect pacing, even a hesitation or two. The theory is that prospects have a "salesperson" pattern-match in their head, and the moment you trigger it, defenses go up. Miner would actually keep something close to my original "feel free to tell me" language — for him that's not a flaw, it's the point. The trade-off: it can read as low-status if the delivery isn't exactly right, which is the risk you just caught.

Grant Cardone style

"[Name], Grant Cardone. Not gonna waste your time — I work with steel distributors on quoting and inventory, and I think there's something here for you. What's better, Tuesday or Wednesday, 15 minutes?"

Cardone opens with zero permission-seeking and zero acknowledgment that the call might be unwanted. Full certainty, states the value immediately, moves straight to scheduling in the same breath. No discovery-first framing at all — the opener is already half a close.

Where that leaves the line we landed on

What we wrote — "I'll be straight with you, this is a cold call... you got 20 seconds?" — is a hybrid, and worth being honest about that: it keeps Miner's disarm (naming the interruption) but borrows Cardone's directness (closed, concrete ask instead of an open door). That's not an accident of taste, it's the right call for this specific situation — pure NEPQ risks sounding tentative on a product with zero proof behind it, and pure Cardone requires a level of certainty about outcomes you can't back up yet with one client mid-implementation. The hybrid is doing real work, not splitting the difference for its own sake.

I'd stay with the hybrid over either pure version. Straight Cardone is the more dangerous of the two here — assumptive momentum works when the confidence is earned by proof, and you don't have that yet.
