## 50ft analysis: 5 target verticals for raw material commodity distribution

Data quality note up front: most "market size" reports for narrow B2B distribution verticals (Grand View, Verified Market, MarketResearchFuture, etc.) are wildly inconsistent with each other — I saw steel service center figures ranging from $750M to $1.4T depending on source, same "market," same year. I'm anchoring on Census/NAICS-sourced numbers wherever they exist (most reliable), and flagging anything that's SEO-mill-derived as directional only.

### Scoring framework

I ranked candidate verticals on four things that actually matter for your model (fragmented SMB distributors, RFQ/quote-heavy sales motion, legacy ERP/manual workflows, inside-sales-friendly cycle — i.e., "looks like steel"):

1. Market size (US distribution/wholesale revenue)
2. Fragmentation (number of establishments — more = bigger inside-sales hiring pool, faster land-and-expand, less enterprise-sales-cycle risk)
3. Tech/digitization gap (lower = more AI OS opportunity)
4. Sales motion fit (quote-driven relationship selling vs. pure commodity-price trading)

### Candidates researched

|---|---|---|---|
| Metal service centers (423510) | ~$80–140B range across sources, noisy | 4,229 companies, ~151,714 employees | Your validated anchor via EOXS/AskCruz |

### Why I screened out farm products and paper

Farm product raw material wholesalers (grain, cotton, livestock, wool) are merchant wholesalers dealing in raw or natural-state agricultural products — but this is largely commodity trading: price is set by exchange/futures markets, margins are thin, and the sales motion is market-timing and logistics, not relationship-based quoting. That's a fundamentally different product than an AI ops layer for a quote-heavy distributor — closer to a trading desk tool than an AskCruz-style OS. Paper is small and structurally declining. Both are weak fits regardless of size.

### My 5 recommended verticals

1. Metals/Steel — keep as-is, already proven, don't re-litigate
2. Building materials & lumber distribution — largest, most fragmented, most directly analogous buyer to steel (contractors/ops managers, quote-to-order cycles, family-owned distributors). Best single next bet.
3. Chemical distribution (industrial/commodity chemicals) — smaller than building materials but high pain (regulatory complexity, blending, batch tracking) means higher willingness to pay per seat
4. A defined sub-segment of industrial/MRO distribution — not "MRO" broadly (too vague a buyer persona), but a specific slice like industrial fasteners & hardware distribution, which behaves like steel: quote-heavy, fragmented, family-owned
5. Electrical equipment & supplies distribution — sizable, fragmented at the independent-distributor tier, though flag: the top of this market (Wesco, Rexel, Graybar) has already invested heavily in digital, so your ICP here needs to stay firmly in the small/regional independent tier, not compete where incumbents have already modernized

Plastics stays where it is — not one of the "5 hot new verticals," just your existing pilot. The 308-company NAICS universe is too narrow to justify a dedicated inside-sales hire on its own; it likely needs to be folded into a broader "chemicals & polymers" motion rather than treated as a standalone vertical hire.

### The thing I'd push back on

You have two vertical-specific inside sales reps in motion (steel via AskCruz, plastics via GFC Intelligence) and neither has closed enough deals yet to tell you whether the JD, the pitch, or the pricing actually works repeatably. Hiring into 5 verticals now means 5x the onboarding overhead, 5x the messaging to iterate, and 5x the ways a bad JD or bad ICP fit burns cash — before you know if the motion works in vertical #1. Against a 12-month, $1M ARR goal, the highest-leverage move is probably: prove repeatable close in steel or building materials first, codify what worked, then replicate — not parallelize 5 unproven motions at once. If there's a reason you need to move on all 5 simultaneously (e.g., a funding or timeline constraint I don't have visibility into), that changes the calculus — but as a default sequencing question, one proven vertical beats five unproven ones.
