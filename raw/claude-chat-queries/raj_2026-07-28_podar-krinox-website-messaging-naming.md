## LOCKED DECISIONS

**Emotional core:** One arc, not three feelings — *competence undermined by bad information, and a craving to feel in command of the business again.*

**Brand persona (Claude's call, Raj deferred):** Trusted senior advisor — calm, direct, sees what others miss. NOT relentless operator or bold pioneer. Buyer already has drive; what he lacks is visibility.

**Enemy framing (Claude's call, Raj deferred):** Oblique, never named. Describe their data reality with enough specificity that the inadequacy of alternatives becomes self-evident. Belief through recognition, not argument.

**CTA:** "Book a fit call" — NOT a waitlist (contradicts a built, revenue-urgent product) and NOT open self-serve (still requires bespoke integration). Framed as qualification/scoping.

**Proof strategy:** Steel-as-credibility-weapon. Built and proven inside one of the most fragmented, custom, undocumented industries in American manufacturing. Frames narrow proof as evidence of range. Subtext for horizontal buyer: "my business is simpler than that."

**Draft hero copy:**
> You built something real. Most of what you know about it still lives in your head, one employee's inbox, and a system nobody fully trusts.
>
> [Name] is the senior advisor that's actually read everything — every order, every call, every email — and tells you the truth about your business before you have to ask.
>
> [Book a fit call]

## WEBSITE BLUEPRINT (15 sections)

Emotional arc: **Recognition → Tension → Clarity → Belief → Confidence → Action**

1. **Nav bar** — wordmark, 3–4 links max, sticky "Book a fit call". Sparse nav = focused company. Persistent CTA because a buyer sold at 30% scroll must act instantly.
2. **Hero** — headline describes THEIR reality not the product; subhead makes the category claim; primary CTA + secondary link. No carousel, no autoplay. Recognition moment: "how do they know that."
3. **Credibility strip** — thin band under hero. Highest-leverage 40px on the page: resolves the skepticism spike a bold claim creates before it hardens into a bounce.
4. **The problem, articulated better than they could** — 3–4 concrete descriptions of fragmented reality. Oblique enemy at work. Also quietly qualifies out poor-fit visitors, protecting delivery capacity.
5. **Why now** — why newly *possible*, not newly desirable. Removes shame about past failed software purchases; urgency without fake scarcity.
6. **What it actually is** — plain paragraph + simple diagram. Deliberately boring clarity; confident people explain simply.
7. **How it works, 3 steps** — connect → ingest → ask. Honest about bespoke onboarding, framed as thoroughness. Shrinks the buyer's private simulation of ERP-rollout pain.
8. **What you can ask it** — 5–8 real example questions as a CEO would type them. Highest-converting section. Pull from REAL client questions; invented ones read too clean.
9. **What makes it different** — exactly 3 differentiators. Handles "how is this different from ChatGPT" without asking it aloud.
10. **Proof and evidence** — steel-forged narrative, one client story, one quote. Placed AFTER differentiation.
11. **Objection handling** — security, data handling, integration, time required. You're asking a CEO to let a system read every email and transaction.
12. **Vision layer** — Company Brain → Hands → Digital Workforce. Placed DEEP: read early it's hype, read late it's trajectory. Where investors scroll. One page, two audiences, resolved by sequence.
13. **Who this is for** — includes who it's NOT for. Status move; operationally deflects poor-fit calls, protecting the 30-onboarding constraint.
14. **Final CTA** — restated claim + button + what the call actually is (scoping, not pitch).
15. **Footer** — GFC relationship, contact, legal, privacy.

**Rules:** CTA minimum 3x (nav, after §8 at peak desire, final). Never before §4. Full page under 3 minutes; each section lands its point in the headline alone.

## DESIGN / AESTHETIC SPEC

**Core correction:** "Expensive" in consumer luxury and enterprise software are opposite aesthetics. Consumer luxury = ornament. Enterprise luxury = restraint, space, precision, silence. If it looks like a watch brand, a CEO reads consultancy, not infrastructure. Target: quiet, dense, exact. Expensive because nothing is wasted.

**Color** — Base near-black #0B0C0E / #101114 (never pure black). Surfaces #16181C, #1E2126 for elevation via background shift, not borders. Text never pure white: #F2F3F5 / #9BA1A8 / #6B7178. Exactly ONE accent, not gold: cold platinum #C8CDD4 or deep ember #C8642A. Accent on under 5% of page. Forbidden: multi-hue gradients, purple-blue AI gradient, glassmorphism, neon.

**Typography** — Two families max. Display: Söhne, Neue Haas Grotesk, GT America, or serif (Editorial New, Signifier). Serif headline + sans body is the most reliable "expensive" B2B move. Body: Inter, Söhne, Suisse International. Headline tracking -0.02 to -0.03em; body line-height 1.6–1.7; body 17–19px; max 3 weights; tabular figures for numbers.

**Space** — Section padding 120–180px. Content ~1100–1200px, text columns 65–70 chars. Asymmetry over centring. Empty space IS the luxury signal.

**Motion** — weight, not animation. cubic-bezier(0.16, 1, 0.3, 1). Never linear, never bounce. 400–700ms entrances, 150–250ms hover. Reveals: fade + 12–20px translate, 60–80ms stagger, trigger 15% into viewport. No scale-on-hover. No custom cursors. Only ~1/3 of sections animate. Respect prefers-reduced-motion.

**Graphics** — NO stock photography ever; no glowing brains or robot hands. (1) product as hero, cropped at section edges [PRIMARY]; (2) data as abstraction — 1px line work, architecture-specific [connective tissue]; (3) real client-facility photography — only if access is genuine.

**Texture** — 2–4% noise overlay. Soft colorless radial glow behind hero. Borders rgba white 6–8%. Corner radius 6–8px.

**The test:** could anything be removed without losing meaning? If yes, remove it.

## NAMING WORKSTREAM — TEN ROUNDS

**Round 1 — industrial metaphors, all collide:** Ingot, Kiln (Kiln AI at kiln.tech), Truss (3 collisions), Keel (London startup, $13.1M, no-code-to-ERP bridge — nearly identical positioning).

**Round 2 — coined:** Veyra (taken), Novira (taken), Corvyn (clean), Tessero (CLEAN).

**Round 3 — KRINOX selected, saved to OV2, Raj said he'd buy krinox.com.** LATER REJECTED.

**Round 4 — two-word movie/history:** Map Room (Churchill's Cabinet War Rooms — strongest meaning), Mission Control, First Mate, High Ground, Round Table, Gordian Knot (Fortive-owned), Ironbridge, Crucible.

**Round 5 — domains:** maproom.ca / prata.ca REJECTED — .ca signals Canadian to US buyers (larger half of ICP), raising data-residency anxiety in the URL; CIRA presence requirements may bar Raj; prata.ca collapses Prata Inc / GFC separation. mymaproom.com REJECTED ("my" is dated, shrinks a command center to a personal dashboard). themaproom.ai GOOD. Ranking: maproom.ai > themaproom.ai > getmaproom.com > maproomhq.com > mymaproom.com.

**Round 6 — CEREBRO / brain anatomy:** Disney is NOT the main risk. Cerebri AI Inc. holds CEREBRI / CEREBRI AI marks covering software for improving business processes via ML, NLP, data integration — identical class. CEREBRAS (Cerebras Systems, S-1 filed April 2026). CEREBRO live/pending, Ontario, software class. Cerebriu (Denmark). "Cereb-" closed. Brain anatomy is the most-mined field in AI naming. Survivors: White Matter, Myelin, Broca, Medulla. Avoid amygdala.

**Round 7 — obscure real words, ALL COLLIDE:** Quipu (5x, incl. a deep-tech startup whose description nearly matches this product's spec), Orrery (Orrery.ai), Alidade (Alidade Technology Inc), Gnomon (Gnomon Technology), Portolan (German ERP company, worst collision), Ephemeris (astrology app + agency + ISP), Vernier (Vernier Science Education, active marks), Exchequer (accounting software), Palimpsest (marketing consultancy).
Conclusion: "obscure real word with a great reveal" is the standard playbook since Palantir (2003); field stripped bare. Coined-from-morphemes offered: Quaestor, Adytum, Tabularium, Veritor, Tessara, Cernos, Anamna.

**Round 8 — all coined Latin REJECTED. Raj: "they do not have any significance... I as a creator won't be able to remember. we need a name that stays with you once you hear it."**

**CRITICAL DIAGNOSIS:** Raj had asked for a name that "makes a person question what it means" AND one that "stays with you once you hear it." These fight each other. Obscure words provoke curiosity precisely because they have no foothold in memory — which is also why those domains were free. Memorability comes from familiarity and concreteness, not rarity.

**THE REFRAMING INSIGHT:** The only name in this venture that ever stuck is *Ganesh Fruit Company* — named for Raj's grandfather's fifty-year fruit business. Significance from heritage and story, not etymology.

**MUNIM proposed** — the bookkeeper in a traditional Indian family business who held the entire operational memory: every customer, every debt, every crate out and rupee back. Literal description of the product; almost certainly a real person who worked for Raj's grandfather. Raj LIKED THE DIRECTION but munim.com/.ai unavailable.

**Round 9 — more Indian-heritage options:**
*Ruled out:* Daftar (means "list" in Indonesian/Malay — fighting a whole language for SEO), Karta (AI customer support company exists), Khata (Khatabook).
*Record-keepers:* **Munshi** (Mughal/colonial clerk who kept books AND wrote all correspondence — the only role doing both records and letters, doubly apt since the product ingests email; no software collision found), Patwari, Kanungo, Bahi.
*Discernment:* **Paras** (the mythical stone that turns iron into gold — two open syllables, easiest US pronunciation of any candidate all session; for a company forged in steel it's a one-sentence unforgettable story), Parakh, Kasauti.
*Witness/account:* Sakshi, Lekha, Hisab.
*Trust:* Hundi (the bill of exchange that moved credit across India for centuries on trust alone).
**Recommendation: PARAS first, MUNSHI second.**

**Round 10 — Raj asked about American surnames and street names.**

*Street names — steered off.* They carry atmosphere, not meaning (Madison, Lexington, LaSalle, Bleecker signal a neighbourhood and price bracket, nothing about the product). They also borrow an identity that isn't Raj's. Would repeat the exact failure he just named — no significance, nothing to remember. Finance-adjacent ones heavily taken.

*Surnames — work only in one form:* when the surname belongs to a real person whose life's work prefigured the product, so the name carries a one-sentence story. Four candidates identified:
- **Sanborn** — the Sanborn Map Company documented every building in every American city, block by block: construction materials, occupancy, contents, hazards. Fire insurers couldn't price risk without it; still the most granular record of American commerce ever assembled. Two syllables, unmistakably American. Meaning: total documentation of what is actually there vs. what people assume.
- **Hollerith** — Herman Hollerith built the punched-card tabulating machine for the 1890 US Census because hand-counting a nation had become impossible; his company became IBM. Literally the man who first made a country's data machine-readable. Three syllables, distinctive, very likely clear on domain and mark.
- **Deming** — W. Edwards Deming taught postwar Japanese manufacturing to manage by measurement rather than opinion. Specific advantage: a meaningful share of the ICP already knows the name, which shortcuts explanation entirely. Caution: Deming is also a city in New Mexico, complicating the domain.
- **Bradstreet** — Lewis Tappan's Mercantile Agency (1841) ran correspondents in towns across America reporting on merchants' finances and character — the first commercial business-intelligence network in the country. Became Dun & Bradstreet, which is the collision risk, but the lineage is exact.

**Claude's read:** Deming and Sanborn strongest; Hollerith most interesting if he wants something unknown that becomes unforgettable after the explanation.

**The larger question posed to Raj:** the heritage direction (Munim/Munshi/Paras) gives a name whose significance is *his*, drawn from a family business he descends from, and pairs with GFC into a founder story investors repeat. An American surname gives legibility with the buyer but borrows someone else's history. Two different bets about whether the brand's meaning comes from the founder or from the market.
